d. What guidance does Wilke give about whether or not to stack bars vs. dodge them (using an offset in Vega-Lite)?

Wilke explains that grouped (dodged) bars are useful when comparing categories across another variable because each bar shares the same baseline, making comparisons easier. Stacked bars are better when the total of the values is meaningful, since the combined bar represents the sum. However, stacked bars make it harder to compare individual segments because they do not share a common baseline.

g. Explain why Figures 6.12 and 6.13 are labeled “bad”.

Figures 6.12 and 6.13 are labeled “bad” for different reasons. Figure 6.12 uses bars to show life expectancy, which is a problem because bars imply the values start from zero. Since all countries have life expectancy between 60–82, the bars are extremely long and nearly the same length, making the differences between countries very hard to see. The dot plot in 6.11 is much better because it doesn’t need to start at zero and lets you focus on the actual differences.

Figure 6.13 is bad because the countries are sorted alphabetically instead of by value. This turns the dots into a scattered, disordered cloud with no clear pattern, making it much harder to compare countries or spot which ones have higher or lower life expectancy.

  1. database people don’t like pie chart because angles are hard to intrepret to see right off the bet what is the highest and lowest.

  2. Healy is more critical of pie charts, saying angles are hard to read and bars are usually better. Wilke is more neutral — he thinks pie charts can actually be the best choice when the dataset is small and you want to show parts of a whole, like the Bundestag example where the pie makes the majority immediately obvious. Both agree pie charts aren’t always wrong, but Healy is quicker to avoid them.

  3. Wilke presents stacked bars and side-by-side bars as alternatives. He favors stacked bars when the total matters and you’re comparing proportions over time. He favors side-by-side bars when you want to directly compare individual values. Pie charts work best for small datasets where you just want to show simple fractions of a whole.

library(vegawidget)

bundestag <- data.frame(
  party = c("CDU/CSU", "SPD", "FDP"),
  seats = c(243, 214, 39),
  order = c(1, 2, 3)
)

  layer = list(
    list(
      mark = list(type = "arc"),

          field = "party",

        )
      )
    ),
    list(
      mark = list(type = "text", radius = 120, fontSize = 13),

        theta = list(field = "seats", type = "quantitative", stack = TRUE),
