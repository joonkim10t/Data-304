I used a scatterplot because it is a natural way to compare Twin A and Twin B directly. Twin A is on the x-axis, Twin B is on the y-axis, and the dashed diagonal line shows where the results would match exactly. Any point away from that line means the kit gave the twins slightly different estimates for the same region. I faceted the chart by kit so each company has its own panel, which makes the figure much easier to read than putting everything into one graph. Most points fall close to the diagonal, which suggests that all three kits give broadly similar results for identical twins. At the same time, the points are not perfectly on the line, showing that small differences still appear across all three kits. This suggests that ancestry percentages should be treated as estimates rather than exact measurements.

Issues with the original graphic

The original chart uses a stacked bar to show phone, chat, and email touchpoints over time. The first thing that stands out is the seasonal pattern — all three channels spike around December and January, dip through spring and summer, and climb again at the end of the year. Email consistently has the highest volume, phone is second, and chat is the smallest. But chat shows the clearest upward trend, starting around 0.11 in early 2018 and reaching 0.26 by January 2020. Phone and email are mostly flat year over year, just cycling through the same seasonal rhythm. So the real story isn’t just that total touchpoints grew slightly — it’s that chat is becoming a more significant part of the overall volume.

Part c – The story

The top panel shows contraception use and unmet need for family planning, while the bottom panel shows the total fertility rate. Over 25 years, modern contraception use in Tanzania went from about 6% to 38% — a massive increase. You’d expect fertility to drop by a similar amount, but it barely moved, going from 6.2 to 5.2 children per woman. That’s a decline, but a surprisingly small one given how much contraception access changed. Meanwhile, the percentage of women with unmet need for family planning stayed stubbornly flat around 22–28%, dipping slightly but never really dropping in a meaningful way. So even though more women are using contraception, there are still a lot of women who want to limit or space their pregnancies but aren’t able to. The gap between rising contraception use and relatively flat fertility suggests that other factors — like desired family size, access to healthcare, and cultural norms — are playing a big role in keeping fertility rates high.

Part b

So the main idea behind this chart was to show that the Golden Boot isn’t just about who scores the most — it’s also about how efficient they were. That’s why I went with bars for total goals and an orange line for goals per game. Like, Haaland scored 36 in 2022-23 which is insane, but Kane’s 29 in 30 games back in 2016-17 was actually a better rate. I thought that was an interesting thing to highlight.

I know dual y-axes can be sketchy in data viz — we talked about how they can be misleading. But here I figured it’s okay because goals and goals-per-game come from the same underlying data, so it’s not like I’m forcing a fake correlation between two random things. Plus the bar vs line distinction makes it pretty obvious which axis goes with which.

Color-wise, I went with the Premier League’s purple for the bars and orange for the line. I actually tried green first but it looked kind of hard to read against the dark purple, so I switched to orange which pops a lot better. The tooltips were a must because otherwise you’d have no idea who each bar is — you just see a height and a season. Hovering lets you see the player, club, goals, games, and the efficiency number.

One thing I had to deal with was seasons where multiple players tied. 2018-19 had three guys all at 22 goals (Aubameyang, Mane, Salah). If I kept all of them it looked messy with bars stacking on top of each other, so I just picked the one with the best goals-per-game and mentioned the ties here.

I thought about doing a scatter plot instead — games on the x-axis, goals on the y — which would show efficiency more directly since the best scorers would be up in the top left. But you lose the timeline that way and I wanted to show how scoring trends changed over the years. I also considered just doing a plain bar chart without the line, but the goals-per-game layer adds something extra without making it too busy.

Exercise 5: Using Your Palette

  1. Encoding channel other than x or y: In my first graphic for Exercise 1, I used color and shape to represent the three genetic kits — each one gets a different color and a different point shape (circle, square, triangle) so you can tell them apart even without the legend. I also used yOffset to spread the twin dots apart within each ancestry region so they don’t just sit on top of each other. And in Exercise 4, color separates the purple bars from the orange line.

  2. Layers: My Exercise 4 chart layers bars and a line on the same plot — bars for total goals, line for goals per game. The twin comparison scatter in Exercise 1 (Graphic 2) also has a layer thing going on, where I put a dashed diagonal reference line behind the actual data points so you can see where perfect agreement would be.

  3. Facets: Exercise 1 uses faceting in both graphics. Graphic 1 facets by twin pair into a 2-column layout, and Graphic 2 facets by kit so each company gets its own scatter panel. I think faceting was the right call for both since cramming everything into one chart would’ve been way too cluttered.

  4. Concatenation: Exercise 3 stacks two panels vertically with vconcat — contraception and unmet need on top, fertility on the bottom. They share the same x-axis so your eyes can just go straight down to compare the trends.

  5. Non-default scale or guide settings: A few places. In Exercise 4 I used independent y-axis resolution so the bars and line get their own scales, and I manually set the goals axis to go up to 45 so everything fits. Exercise 1 Graphic 1 has the x-axis domain locked to 0-1 with percentage formatting. Exercise 2 has manually assigned colors for each channel instead of just letting Vega-Lite pick defaults.

  6. Tooltips: Pretty much every graphic has them. The most important ones are probably in Exercise 4 since you literally can’t tell who each bar represents without hovering — it shows the player, club, goals, games, and goals-per-game ratio.

  7. Another kind of interaction: Honestly I didn’t go crazy with this one. I have code-fold turned on everywhere so the code stays hidden unless someone clicks to expand it, which keeps the page clean. I thought about adding brushing or a slider to filter by season range in Exercise 4, but it felt like overkill for this particular chart — the tooltips already give you what you need.
